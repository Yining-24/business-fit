# Research and economics
Adapted and rewritten methods; attribution and changes: [sources](sources.md).

## Evidence and customer synthesis
Maintain a small evidence ledger: claim | fact/report/estimate/assumption/unknown | source or participant ID | date | segment | confidence and limitation. Attribute confidence to evidence quality, not confidence of prose.

With actual notes, extract observations separately from interpretation; code them into themes, review outliers, and compare segments and contradictory sources. Count unique participants supporting a theme with a denominator, not repeated mentions. Retain source IDs and exact quotes only if present. Report recruitment and sample bias; interview frequency is not population prevalence. If no raw research exists, create interview questions and a plan, never a synthesized customer finding.

## Competition and demand
Define the customer, geography and decision. Compare a few relevant direct providers, different approaches, adjacent suppliers, hiring/outsourcing, DIY and doing nothing. Use current official offer/pricing pages for commercial facts; independent reviews and communities for reported experiences, with bias caveats. Record price units, taxes/fees if material, currency, billing period and access date. Unknown or unpublished prices stay unknown.

Compare buyer-important outcomes, delivery, trust, access channels and switching costs. Separate available capabilities from planned ones and unsupported absence claims. Treat an apparent gap as a hypothesis: it may mean low demand or expensive delivery. Explain a plausible reason customers would change and how the user could reach them; competitor existence is category evidence, not proof of demand for this offer.

## Size reachable demand
Use explicit customer segment and horizon. Estimate eligible buyers × plausible purchase frequency × price, then constrain by channel reach, conversion, sales cycle, hours and delivery capacity. Show source and assumption for each input. Use ranges or scenarios; do not use 'just 1%' or a default penetration rate. TAM/SAM/SOM are optional when useful; for a small operation, attainable customer count and required volume often matter more than a global market report.

## Economics appropriate to the model
Choose a unit (job, class, item, customer-month) and consistent time period/currency. Include materials, fulfillment, platform/payment fees, refunds, support, acquisition and labor. Separate cash expenses from the economic cost of the owner's time; avoid double counting labor.

- Contribution per unit = unit price − variable cost per unit.
- Cash operating surplus = units × cash contribution − fixed cash costs.
- Economic surplus also deducts owner time at an explicit assumed rate if not already included.
- Break-even units = fixed costs / positive contribution per unit; zero/negative contribution has no finite break-even at those assumptions.
- Required units for a cash income goal = (fixed cash costs + desired pre-tax owner cash income) / cash contribution. Compare with available hours, acquisition and capacity. Clarify whether the user's goal is revenue, pre-tax income or take-home pay.
- CAC = attributable acquisition spend / new customers; show owner acquisition time separately. Zero paid spend does not mean acquisition has no cost.
- For recurring models only, scenario LTV ≈ contribution per customer per period / churn per same period, with stable retention and margin assumptions stated. No evidence-backed churn means unknown LTV or explicitly hypothetical scenarios. CAC payback = CAC / positive periodic contribution, with units shown.

Show conservative/base/optimistic scenarios only as assumptions, not forecasts. Vary the inputs most likely to change the decision; identify price, cost, volume or utilization breakpoints. Do not impose universal SaaS margins, churn defaults or LTV:CAC thresholds on unrelated businesses. Workaround cost informs value; it is not an absolute price ceiling. Include startup cash, working capital, time to cash and practical operating requirements where material. Distinguish contribution, revenue, cash flow and profit.
